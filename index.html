Take fewer pictures, get the same cell.

A Raman microscope records, for every point of a cell, a colour-fingerprint of its molecules (a spectrum). Scanning point by point is slow. Here the cell is lit with patterns of light instead, and a computer works the image back out. Everything on this page is a simulation you can change.

A hyperspectral cube has two image directions and one spectrum direction. These surfaces show where the signal is bright inside that cube.

Live lab

Everything here is computed in your browser, right now. Move a slider and the cell is lit, photographed (with realistic noise) and rebuilt again.

Click any picture of the cell to choose the pixel whose spectrum is drawn. The wavenumber slider picks which colour-slice of the spectrum cube you look at.

Which light patterns are best?

Nine ways of lighting the sample, compared at a fixed total measuring time: more patterns means each one gets less light.

Does mixing pixels in one shot help?

Lighting many pixels at once collects more light per shot. It only pays off when the camera itself is noisy.

Which way of rebuilding the image?

Same measurements, eight different reconstruction recipes. Some of them are told what the molecules look like in advance; that is an optimistic assumption, marked below.

How sure can we be?

Repeat the experiment with fresh noise and see how much the answer wobbles, which patterns are numerically fragile, and which fine details survive.

When the sample moves

Movement between shots is usually a nuisance. If you know how far it moved, it can become part of the code.

Where the noise numbers come from

The camera noise in every experiment was measured from repeated photographs of a real single-pixel imaging dataset (SPIHIM), not invented.
